INSURANCE TERMS GLOSSARY
==========================

This file covers key insurance terminology relevant to working in the insurance industry.

Sum Insured (SI):
The maximum amount an insurer will pay in the event of a claim.
It represents the financial value of the insured asset or risk.

Indemnity SI:
Insurance based on the principle of indemnity — the insured is restored to the
same financial position they were in before the loss, but no more.
The payout reflects the actual loss suffered, not the original or replacement value.

Replacement SI:
Insurance where the payout covers the full cost to REPLACE the insured item with
a new equivalent — regardless of depreciation or age.
More expensive than Indemnity SI but provides better coverage.

LOI (Letter of Intent / Limit of Indemnity):
Can refer to:
  - Limit of Indemnity: the maximum amount the insurer will pay per claim or in total
  - Letter of Intent: a document expressing intent to proceed with a transaction or agreement

Excess:
The amount the policyholder must pay FIRST before the insurer contributes to a claim.
Also called a "deductible" in some regions.
Example: If the excess is $500 and the claim is $3,000, the insurer pays $2,500.

Deductible:
Same concept as Excess (used more commonly in the USA).
The fixed amount the insured pays before insurance coverage kicks in.

Excess vs Deductible:
Both mean the policyholder pays the first portion of a claim.
"Excess" is the term used more in Australia/UK; "Deductible" in the USA.

Retroactive Date:
The date from which an insurance policy's coverage begins looking backwards.
Any events occurring BEFORE the retroactive date are not covered, even if the
policy is active when the claim is made.
Common in professional indemnity (PI) and D&O policies.

Runoff:
Coverage that extends beyond the policy expiry date to cover claims made after
the policy ends but relating to events that occurred DURING the policy period.
Important in professional indemnity, medical malpractice, and D&O insurance.

GL — General Liability:
Insurance that protects a business against third-party claims for bodily injury,
property damage, or personal injury caused by the business's operations.

EL — Employers Liability:
Insurance that covers a business against claims from employees who are injured
or become ill as a result of their work.

SL — (may refer to) Statutory Liability:
Coverage for fines and penalties resulting from unintentional breaches of legislation.

PI — Professional Indemnity:
Insurance that protects professionals against claims of negligence, errors, or
omissions in their professional services.
Common for consultants, lawyers, accountants, IT firms.

Malicious Damage:
Coverage for damage intentionally caused by a person, as opposed to accidental damage.
Some policies specifically include or exclude malicious damage.

Coinsurance:
A clause requiring the policyholder to insure their asset for at least a minimum
percentage of its true value. If underinsured, the insurer reduces the payout proportionally.
Example: If you insure a $1M asset for only $500K (50%), and suffer a $200K loss,
the insurer may only pay $100K (50%).

Reinsurance:
Insurance bought BY insurance companies to protect themselves from large claims.
When a major event occurs (like a natural disaster), the reinsurer covers a portion
of the insurer's losses, spreading risk across the market.

Quotation:
A formal offer from an insurer specifying the terms, conditions, and premium
for providing insurance coverage. Not yet a binding contract.

Schedule:
The specific details of a policy: the insured's name, property/risk description,
sum insured, premium, and key dates. Part of the formal policy documents.

Certificate of Insurance:
A document that provides evidence that insurance coverage exists.
Often requested by clients or third parties as proof of insurance.
Not the full policy — just a summary certificate.

Wording:
The formal policy document containing the full terms, conditions, exclusions,
and definitions of the insurance contract.
The "wording" is the legal basis of what is and isn't covered.

Peril:
A specific risk or cause of loss that an insurance policy covers.
Examples: fire, flood, theft, accidental damage, earthquake.
Named perils policies cover only the listed perils; all-risks policies cover
everything except specific exclusions.
